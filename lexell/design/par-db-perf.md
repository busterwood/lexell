# Parallel map against lexdb's CPU-bound work: what it would buy

Status: experiment, 2026-11-14, on `exp/par-db-perf` (a worktree of `main`
at 114131f1). Nothing is merged. The kernels are in `bench/par_db.lxl` and
`bench/par_copy.lxl`; the driver is `bench/par_db_run.py`; the raw results
are in `bench/par_db_results/`.

## 0. The comparison this revisits

The user remembered two comparisons where lexdb's single thread was the
stated limit. Both were found:

**COPY** ([lexdb.md](lexdb.md) 9.118 and 9.119). The wide 1M-row load was
5.15 s, then 3.15 s against PostgreSQL 18's 1.96 s. 9.118 says:

> lexdb took 2.2 to 4.7 times PostgreSQL's time. A load runs on one thread
> and is bound by its CPU.

and its first not-yet-done item is:

> Lines parsed and rows encoded on 3 or 4 threads while one inserts.
> PostgreSQL's COPY uses one thread, so this is where lexdb could pass it.

**Aggregates and hash joins** ([lexdb-engines.md](lexdb-engines.md) 3E and
6). 3E says PostgreSQL's parallel workers are why its wall times for scans,
aggregates and joins are about half its CPU, and:

> lexdb's single SQL thread is a design choice (lexdb.md section 0); this is
> left out of the plan below.

Section 6 measured lexdb one thread against PostgreSQL at four processes
(the leader and three workers, the user's 2026-11-13 rule). PostgreSQL ran
2.5 to 5 times faster where aggregates and hash joins split across workers:
two-column DISTINCT 4.8x, GROUP BY text 4.1x, `w join t` 3.5x, a text-keyed
join count 3.2x, windows 2.6x, DISTINCT 2.5x. It ends:

> Closing those last needs lexdb to run one query on several cores (scans
> and partial aggregates split by key range), not only a faster single
> thread.

So the user's memory is right, and the two cases have different answers:
COPY has a single-threaded competitor, so parallel lexdb can pass it;
aggregates have a competitor that also scales, so parallel lexdb can narrow
the gap but not close it unless its per-core work is also fixed.

## 1. Method

Machine: Ryzen, 8 cores and 16 hardware threads, Linux, load 0.9 to 1.2
during the reported runs; other sessions sometimes took three cores, which
is why the 8-thread numbers have the wider spread. `lxlc -O`; native
backend. `LXL_PAR=0, 1, 3, 7`
means 1, 2, 4 and 8 threads (the pool's N workers plus the calling thread).
Times are the program's own monotonic clock around the kernel, median of 3
to 5 rounds; the first round is dropped because the pool starts lazily.
Reading the input, building the rows and dropping them are outside the
timer. User time is from `getrusage(RUSAGE_CHILDREN)`.

The pool start is a one-time cost and below the resolution here: on the
first versus later rounds, COPY parse+encode gave 174, 175, 179, 176,
174 ms at 4 threads and 129, 124, 123, 133, 131 ms at 8. A long-running
server pays it once and keeps the pool.

The data:
- `par_db.lxl`: 1M rows built in memory, `a` in 0..999, one of 20 city
  names, a `Decimal` price.
- `par_copy.lxl`: the wide 12-column file of 9.118, generated by
  `saved/results/2026-11-13-copy/gen.py wide 1000000 w1m.tsv`
  (167 MB). It splits, parses and encodes each line; it does not insert.
- PostgreSQL 18.6, the saved `saved/pg18` data directory, warm cache,
  `shared_buffers=1GB`. One core is `max_parallel_workers_per_gather=0`.
  Four processes is `max_parallel_workers_per_gather=3` and
  `max_parallel_workers=3` (leader plus three workers). Wall ms, median of
  three, output to `/dev/null`.

The two `par_copy` families are deliberate:
- `parseN`/`allN` use `strings.splitByte`, whose fields are owned `str`
  values, as the current `textRecord` makes `Val.Text` values;
- `parseBN`/`borrowN` use `strings.split`, whose fields are `&str` views of
  the line, and the closure takes `lp: &[str]` rather than the owned slice.
  This is the user's borrow tip: no count changes per field.

## 2. Kernel speed-ups

**Aggregation and expression kernels**, 1M rows, ms (median; speed-up in
parentheses). `group1` is one Map over all rows, the current shape;
`groupN` is 16 chunk Maps merged on the caller.

| kernel | 1 thread | 2 | 4 | 8 | user CPU at 8 vs 1 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `groupN` (GROUP BY text, count+sum) | 38 | 19 (2.0x) | 10 (3.8x) | 5 (7.6x) | 0.26 / 0.25 s |
| `distinctN` (two-column DISTINCT) | 80 | 41 (2.0x) | 21 (3.8x) | 11 (7.3x) | 0.47 / 0.46 s |
| `joinN` (1M probes of a 20-key text Map) | 18 | 9 (2.0x) | 4 (4.2x) | 2 (7.2x) | 0.15 / 0.15 s |
| `exprN` (`a+b`, `price*2`, `upper(city)`) | 144 | 86 (1.7x) | 46 (3.1x) | 32 (4.5x) | 1.07 / 0.78 s |

Chunking costs 0 to 5% at one thread (`group1` 38 against `groupN` 38;
`expr1` 137 against `exprN` 144): 16 maps are enough at 4 to 8 threads.
For COPY, 4 chunks limit 8 threads to 170 ms; 16, 64 and 256 are all 87 to
91 ms at 8 threads and 169 to 172 ms at 4. Sixteen is enough.

**COPY: split, parse and encode**, 1M wide rows, ms.

| work | 1 | 2 | 4 | 8 | 8-thread speed-up |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| split only, owned fields (`splitN`) | 146 | 198 | 144 | 84 | 1.7x |
| split only, borrowed fields (`splitBN`) | 96 | 50 | 26 | 12 | 8.0x |
| split+parse, owned (`parseN`) | 493 | 318 | 160 | 89 | 5.5x |
| split+parse, borrowed (`parseBN`) | 482 | 242 | 125 | 62 | 7.8x |
| split+parse+encode, owned (`allN`) | 698 | 432 | 219 | 124 | 5.6x |
| split+parse+encode, borrowed (`borrowN`) | 634 | 332 | 166 | 86 | 7.4x |
| the same as one loop, borrowed (`borrow1`) | 638 | | | | |

Two results:
- **Borrowed fields are cheaper before parallelism.** Splitting alone fell
  from 146 to 96 ms, and the whole parse+encode from 702 to 638 ms (9%).
- **They scale much better.** Owned splitting barely improves (1.7x at 8
  threads) because every field is a counted `str`; borrowed splitting is
  8.0x. The full borrow kernel is 3.8x at 4 threads and 5.7 to 7.4x at 8;
  the owned one is 3.2x and 5.6x. Worker user CPU is only 3 to 5% above
  one thread at 4 threads, and about 6 to 16% at 8.

The 8-thread variance (86 to 132 ms across runs) is from the machine, not
the code; the 4-thread numbers are stable.

## 3. What the real lexdb profiles say

Profiles are `perf -e cpu-clock` on the server, resolved against
`LXL_NATIVE_MAP`; top 100 symbols, grouped by hand. The categories are
approximate to 3 to 5 points.

**Wide COPY** (3.3 s, 11,992 samples):

| part | share |
|---|---:|
| parse (`coerce`, `coerceZ`, `parseIntOpt`, `uuidBytes`, `parseUuid`, `parseNum`, `flt_parse`, `fastTs`, `textRecord`, `strings.trim`, decimal parse) | 30.6% |
| encode (`encFixed`, `kindsFit`, `wordOf`, `placeKind`, `binary.append*`, `varBytes`, `fixedSize`, `Decimal.toBin`) | 15.0% |
| allocation and counts (`dup`, `drop`, `malloc`, `memcpy`, `realloc`) | 19.2% |
| **parse + encode + allocation** | **64.8%** |
| store and transaction (`insertOne`, `Map.find`, `Db.descend`, `logWrites`, `memcmp`, `Store.getIn`, `storeRow`, `putValue`, `commit`) | 28.7% |
| checks, `copyStmt`, other | 6.1% |

So about **65% can move to workers**, and **35% cannot** without changing
the store or the transaction. This matches 9.119's prose, which accounts
for the store, key read, counts/allocation and `insertOne` as the
remainder.

**`grp_text`** (`select city, count(*), avg(price) from w group by city`,
190 ms, 858 samples):

| part | share |
|---|---:|
| grouping and aggregation (`bRunInto` 13.1, `arith` 10.4, `bAccVal` 4.9, `batchGroups` 4.9, `asNum`, `makeDec`, `addExact`, SipHash, CRC32C, Map find) | 66.7% |
| scan and decode (`newBatch`, `decodeNode`, `Node.valView`, `lazyLeaf`, `loadAhead`, `snapDescend`, `fixedVal`, `varVal`, `Decimal.fromBin`, `kindOf`) | 29.5% |
| other | 3.8% |

The aggregate itself is the parallel part; the scan and decode are on the
main thread unless raw column bytes are handed to the kernel.

**Other queries**, from the same top-100 grouping:
- `join_text_agg` (110 ms): row work (`bProbe`, `bGather`, hash, Map find,
  `bAccVal`) 50%; scan/decode 36%; other 14%. Parallel fraction 0.5 to
  0.65 depending on whether decode moves.
- `expr_mix` (450 ms): row work (`bGenVals`, `arith`, `Decimal.format`,
  `call1`, `upper`, decimal mul) 35%; scan/decode 26%; wire/output 17%;
  other 22% (mostly decimal and allocation, also row work). Parallel
  fraction about 0.55 to 0.6; the wire write stays on the main thread.
- `scan9` (`select *`, 520 ms CPU, 793 ms wall): wire/output 38%;
  scan/decode 36%; row work 17%; other 10%. If decode stays serial, only
  0.27 is parallel; if it moves, 0.63.
- `window` (230 ms): window partitioning and values, sort and selection
  are most of it; wire is 5%. Parallel fraction about 0.7.

## 4. Projected wall times

Amdahl, with `p` the parallel fraction, `s` the measured kernel speed-up,
and the serial part `1 - p`:

    speed-up = 1 / ((1 - p) + p / s)

These are projections, not measurements of an integrated lexdb. They assume
results are handed over for free (the pool's owner tags do that), and that
the caller's merge is small.

**COPY**, 3.31 s measured here (PG 1.42 s here; 1.96 s in 9.119):

| design | p | s at 4 | s at 8 | projected 4 | projected 8 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| batched parse+encode, owned fields | 0.65 | 3.2 | 5.6 | 1.81x, 1.83 s | 2.15x, 1.54 s |
| batched parse+encode, borrowed fields | 0.65 | 3.8 | 7.4 | 1.92x, 1.72 s | 2.28x, 1.45 s |
| pipelined: parse while inserting | 0.65 | 3.8 | 7.4 | insert-bound, 1.16 s | insert-bound, 1.16 s |

The batch version approximately levels with PostgreSQL here, and is 1.1 to
1.3x faster than the 1.96 s of 9.119. The pipelined version is the one that
passes PostgreSQL on this machine: at 4 threads the parse (0.65/3.8 = 0.17
of the old time) is below the store's 0.35, so the load becomes
insert-bound. That needs the insert to run concurrently with the next
chunk's parse, through a task or a producer/consumer pair; a single
`par.map` call parks the caller and cannot overlap the two.

Two details:
- This split is the wide load with a primary key and no secondary index.
  The narrow load is mostly the store (9.119: the commit's puts 28%, the
  key's read 14%), so a parallel COPY would barely improve it. With two
  secondary indexes the store's share is larger and the gain smaller.
- The 65% includes the allocation and count work of parsing; the measured
  kernel speed-ups already include those, and the worker paths are dearer,
  so the 3.8x and 7.4x (or 5.6x with owned fields) are the honest numbers,
  not 4x and 8x.
- Borrowed fields also cut the serial part by 9% (702 to 638 ms of
  parse+encode); the table uses the borrowed 3.31 s base.

**Aggregates and row-returning queries**, ms. "Only row work" leaves scan
and decode serial; "+ decode" assumes the kernel receives raw column bytes
and decodes them. PostgreSQL's four processes are on the same machine.

| query | lexdb 1 | p | s 4 / 8 | projected 4 | projected 8 | PG 1 | PG 4 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `group by city, avg(price)` | 190 | 0.67 / 0.87 | 3.8 / 7.6 | 96 / 68 | 81 / 52 | 140 | 45 |
| `distinct city` | 100 | 0.70 | 3.8 / 7.2 | 45 | 32 | 105 | 36 |
| `distinct a, city` | 210 | 0.70 | 3.8 / 7.2 | 94 | 66 | 125 | 45 |
| text-keyed hash join, count | 110 | 0.55 / 0.65 | 4.2 / 7.2 | 65 / 58 | 51 / 44 | 95 | 35 |
| `expr_mix` (1M rows out) | 450 | 0.55 | 3.1 / 4.5 | 265 | 219 | 459 | 459 |
| `select *` | 520 | 0.27 / 0.63 | 3.1 / 4.5 | 426 / 335 | 397 / 306 | 583 | 568 |
| `window` | 230 | 0.70 | 3.8 / 7.2 | 103 | 73 | 163 | 99 |
| `order by b` | 80 | - | - | - | - | 706 | 594 |
| `count(*)` | 20 | - | - | - | - | 38 | 19 |

The pattern:
- **Aggregates and text hash joins:** parallel lexdb is 1.2 to 2.1 times
  behind PostgreSQL's four processes even after the best case. PostgreSQL
  also scales (3.1x on `group by city`, 3.4x on the text group), and its
  one-core time is already faster. Parallelism narrows the gap; it does
  not close it. Closing it needs the single-core work too (text hashing,
  decimal formatting, scan/decode).
- **Row-returning queries:** `expr_mix`, `select *`, and windows move
  lexdb ahead, because PostgreSQL's plan stays serial for a 1M-row result
  (459 and 568 ms at four processes, against 459 and 583 at one). Even the
  conservative `select *` projection (426 ms at 4 threads) is 1.3x ahead;
  `expr_mix` is 1.7x ahead. This is the surprise: parallelism pays most
  where PostgreSQL cannot use it.
- **Sort:** lexdb's typed sort is already 7 to 9x ahead; parallelising it
  is not the priority.

## 5. Kernel purity: what the check refused, and what is safe

Three refusals came up while writing the COPY kernel. They are recorded
because they are small, provable special cases (and one that should not be
a special case).

**1. Float bit moves: allow them.**

`encFixed`/`wordOf` and `lexdb_float.parse` (through `round4`) reach
`math.bitsOf`/`fromBits`, which are externs:

```
par.map: the closure is not a kernel: it can reach the C function lxllib_float_bits
    encodeAll ...
    bitsOf (math.lxl:147)
    -> the C function lxllib_float_bits
```

The runtime's whole implementation (native/rt.c:698-699) is:

```c
i64 lxllib_float_bits(double x) { union { double d; i64 b; } u; u.d = x; return u.b; }
double lxllib_float_from_bits(i64 b) { union { double d; i64 b; } u; u.b = b; return u.d; }
```

This one is provably safe: no allocation, no state, no I/O, no thread-local
access: as pure as `copysign` and `fabs`, which are already allowed. The
compiler side is one line in `compiler/kernel.lxl`'s `externAllowed`:

```lxl
"lxllib_float_bits" { true } "lxllib_float_from_bits" { true }
```

A temporary patch of that line, built into a self-hosted compiler, was
verified: a kernel that calls both `math.bitsOf` and `lexdb_float.parse`
compiled and ran, and a probe round-tripping four floats through
`fromBits(bitsOf(x))` gave the same values at `LXL_PAR=0` and `3`. The patch
is not committed here because changing the compiler also means reseeding
`seed/lxlc.c`; it is recorded here so the change can be made together with
its test. `lexdb_float.parse` has no other extern, so this is the whole
blocker for double parsing and encoding.

**2. TSTZ's `now`: do not allow the clock; split the fast path.**

`parseTstz` reaches:

```
parseTstz (lexdb_time.lxl:809)
nowTstz (lexdb_time.lxl:398)
now (time.lxl:29)
-> the C function lxl_clock_ns
```

The reach is through the word `now` in the general parser. A kernel's
result would then depend on when it ran, and the pure path does not need
it. Do not allow `lxl_clock_ns`. Instead expose the fast ISO path that
`COPY` already uses: `lexdb_time.fastTs` is private today; a public
`fn parseTsPlain(s: &str): Option[int]` (or `pub fastTs`) that handles
`YYYY-MM-DD[ T]HH:MM:SS[.ffffff][offset]` and returns `None` for words,
zones, `now`, `today` and the rest lets the worker parse the ordinary
values and the caller run the general parser for the rare rest. This is a
correctness improvement too: the fast path is what 9.119 added.

**3. `Zone` in a kernel: its drop reaches Shared/Frozen.**

`parseDate(&s, &z)` is refused even for a date-only string, because the
kernel must own and drop the `Zone` argument:

```
the drop of Zone
the drop of Option[Frozen[Zone]]
the drop of Frozen[Zone]
the drop of Shared[Zone]
-> a Shared or Frozen value
```

The zone rules are behind a `Shared`/`Frozen` registry, and a worker
changing those counts could race. Keep `Zone` out of kernels. A date-only
fast path (`fastTs(s, true)` with no zone) is pure and fits COPY; the
general path with zone names stays on the caller.

In one table:

| reached | why it is refused | safe for a kernel? | what to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| `lxllib_float_bits`, `lxllib_float_from_bits` (via `math.bitsOf`/`fromBits`, `lexdb_float.parse`, `encFixed`) | C functions not on the allowlist | **yes**: inline union bit casts, no state or allocation | add both to `externAllowed`; one line, with a test |
| `lxl_clock_ns` (via `parseTstz` → `nowTstz` → `time.now`) | C function | no: the result depends on when the kernel ran, and the fast path does not need it | do not allow; split the plain ISO fast path from the general parser |
| `rt_sh_dup`/`rt_sh_drop` (via the drop of a `Zone`) | a `Shared`/`Frozen` value | not proven: the zone registry's counts could race | keep `Zone` out of kernels; a date-only fast path needs no zone |

**Also found, but not a purity issue:** owned fields. `strings.splitByte`
returns `[]str` and `Val.Text` values are counted `str`s; a kernel that
splits 12 fields per line changes 12 counts per row. `strings.split`
returns `[]&str`, a borrow of the line, and the same work changes none.
That is the 146 to 96 ms serial and 1.7x to 8.0x scaling above. The COPY
parser can keep borrowed field views and materialise bytes only when it
writes the row (which `encFixed` does anyway).

## 6. What this suggests

1. **Do the cheap purity special cases first.** Allow the two float bit
   moves (one line, probe above). Split the plain ISO timestamp/date fast
   path from the general parser; keep clocks and `Zone` out of kernels.
   These are prerequisites for a COPY kernel, and each is small.
2. **COPY is the best target.** Use borrowed fields, 16 chunks, and a
   batched parse+encode kernel. Expect about 1.7 s at 4 threads or 1.5 s
   at 8 from 3.31 s, roughly level with PostgreSQL here. If that works,
   pipeline the store insert with the next chunk's parse: the load becomes
   insert-bound at about 1.2 s, which beats PostgreSQL's single-threaded
   COPY on either machine. The pipeline needs tasks or a producer/consumer
   handoff; one `par.map` call cannot overlap the two.
3. **Aggregates: parallel partial aggregation over batches, merge on the
   caller.** Expect 2 to 3x, which takes `group by city` from 190 to 68 to
   96 ms, still behind PostgreSQL's 45 ms at four processes. Parallelism
   is necessary but not sufficient there; text hashing, decimal work and
   scan/decode remain. The same applies to two-column DISTINCT and the
   text-keyed hash join.
4. **Prioritise row-returning work where PostgreSQL stays serial.**
   `expr_mix` and `select *` are where parallel lexdb gets ahead of
   PostgreSQL in wall time (1.3 to 2.3x), because PostgreSQL's plan does
   not split a 1M-row result. The wire write is serial in lexdb too, so
   the gain is bounded by the row work and decode that can move.
5. **Do not parallelise the sort or point lookups.** The sort is already
   far ahead; point lookups are store-bound. DML has the COPY shape
   (parse/coerce can move, the store cannot), so COPY is the template.
6. **Cost model.** At 0.8 to 1.6 µs a call, `par.map` suits kernels of tens
   of microseconds or more. Batches of about 1,000 rows are ample; 16
   chunks for a 1M-row load are enough (4 chunks starve 8 threads). The
   pool is lazy and persists, so a server pays the start once.

## 7. Reproduction

Build the compiler (`lexell/compiler/build.sh`). The float-bits probe needs
the one-line allowlist patch of section 5 applied first, then a self-hosted
compiler:

```sh
cd lexell
LXL_LIB=$PWD/lib compiler/out/lxlc compiler/main.lxl --c -o /tmp/lxlc2
LXL_LIB=$PWD/lib /tmp/lxlc2 probe.lxl -O -o /tmp/probe
```

where `probe.lxl` maps `fn(x: &float): float { return math.fromBits(math.bitsOf(x)) }`
over a `[]float` and compares the results with the input, at `LXL_PAR=0`
and `3`. A committed change would also run `compiler/reseed.sh` and add
that program as a `lib/tests/run` test.

Generate the COPY data with the 9.118 generator:

```sh
python3 ~/Work/lexdb-bench/saved/results/2026-11-13-copy/gen.py wide 1000000 w1m.tsv
```

Run the kernels (builds both programs, 5 rounds, writes a TSV):

```sh
python3 lexell/bench/par_db_run.py kernels 5 lexell/bench/par_db_results/par_db_kernels.tsv
python3 lexell/bench/par_db_run.py copy w1m.tsv 3 lexell/bench/par_db_results/par_copy.tsv 16
```

PostgreSQL, from the saved directory (a copy of it was used here; do not
write to the saved one):

```sh
cp -a ~/Work/lexdb-bench/saved/pg18 pgdata
postgres -D pgdata -p 55443 -k /tmp -c listen_addresses=127.0.0.1 \
  -c shared_buffers=1GB -c max_parallel_workers_per_gather=0 &
# one core; for four processes use 3 and max_parallel_workers=3
python3 lexell/bench/par_db_results/pgmeasure.py 55443 queries.txt 3
```

`bench/par_db_results/` holds `par_db_kernels.tsv`, `par_copy.tsv`,
`lex_1thread.tsv`, `pg_1core.tsv`, `pg_4proc.tsv` and `copy.txt`. The
profile inputs (`*.ips`, `*.prof.txt`) and the PostgreSQL data copy are in
`~/Work/lexdb-bench/par-perf/`.

## 8. Caveats

- These are kernel benchmarks, not an integrated lexdb. The store read,
  the wire write and the handover of worker results are measured only
  through their shares in the profiles.
- `par.map` cannot read the store, so a worker cannot scan or insert. The
  projections label the two cases: scan/decode serial, or raw column bytes
  handed to the kernel. The first is what can be built with the current
  rules; the second needs an executor change.
- The profile categories are from the top 100 symbols and hand grouping;
  ±3 to 5 points. The machine had other load (0.9 to 5 during the
  experiments; the reported runs were 0.9 to 1.2 where noted). PostgreSQL's
  numbers here are wall time from the same hour and include the client's
  transfer; 9.119's PostgreSQL figures are not directly comparable, so
  compare within this note.
- PostgreSQL was given four processes, the user's rule, not all 16
  hardware threads. Its COPY is one thread.
- The 65% COPY parallel fraction includes allocation and counts; the
  measured kernel speed-ups already include worker allocation, so the
  projections do not double-count them.
- The aggregate microbenchmark uses `Map.atOrPut` with a `str` key, which
  copies the key on every row; lexdb's batch path carries a group id in an
  array and copies a key only for a new group. The microbenchmark's
  absolute time is therefore a little heavier than lexdb's aggregate
  kernel, and its scaling is representative rather than exact.
